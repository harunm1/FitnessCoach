## Papers

1. **Srinivas, Xu, Liu, Ayush, Galatzer-Levy, Patel et al. (2025).** Substance over style: Evaluating proactive conversational coaching agents. _ACL 2025_. doi:10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1017, https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19328. Read: full text. Five agents: a Directive agent, and Base and Expert versions of an Interrogative and a Facilitative flow; the styles change when advice is given, not only the wording. N = 31 of 37 recruited, 155 conversations (31 x 5 agents). Question-only styles typically reduce engagement and satisfaction, and stylistic components without the core components (context gathering, a relevant recommendation) are viewed negatively. Facilitative was ranked first by 67.7% of users; the Directive agent won 41.94% of pairwise comparisons against 61.29% for Expert-Facilitative and 58.06% for Base-Facilitative. Users with moderate to extensive chatbot experience (3 to 5 on the scale) picked the Directive agent first more often than users with less (1 to 2): 29.2% vs 0%; the authors suspect familiarity bias.
2. **Jörke, Sapkota, Warkenthien, Vainio, Schmiedmayer, Brunskill et al. (2025).** GPTCoach: Towards LLM-based physical activity coaching. _CHI 2025_. doi:10.1145/3706598.3713819. Read: our full note is `2025-jorke-gptcoach.md`; the check read the arXiv version (2405.06061). Design principles: facilitative and non-prescriptive, tailored with context, supportive and non-judgmental tone. In the formative study many participants liked an AI that adapts its persona; one (P13) said: "depending on the day, I will need a different person. Sometimes I'm going to need that empathetic, but other times I'll need the strict regimen."
3. **Hoang, Rogers and Ross (2026).** An LLM-based motivation-aware framework for AI coaching for behaviour change. _CHI 2026_. doi:10.1145/3772318.3791123. Read: abstract level only in the check. The abstract describes motivational support for ambiguous (ambivalent) clients and planning strategies for action-ready ones, and reports significant gains in readiness to change against a baseline system. In the qualitative analysis, users who agreed a change plan with the agent showed the largest intrinsic-motivation gains; that is not an effect size.
4. **Silacci, Cherubini, Boldi, Rapp and Caon (2026).** When workout buddies are virtual: AI agents and human peers in a longitudinal physical activity study. _CHI 2026_. doi:10.1145/3772318.3790303, https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01918. Read: full text. Six-month randomised trial, N = 280, four conditions: human peer, AI peer with a human-like avatar, AI peer with a cyborg avatar, and no peer. The working-alliance bond (the only alliance subscale measured) was higher for both AI peers than for human peers (p < .001); human peers had higher social presence. The two AI conditions did not differ on relatedness, social presence or alliance; the human peer beat the cyborg-avatar AI peer on weekly steps at deployment and follow-up. The authors say believability rested on behavioural coherence, not appearance. Human peers were valued for comparison and accountability, AI peers for steady, low-stakes encouragement; some participants liked the simulated competition, others could not credit an AI with real effort.
5. **Cox, Wester and van Berkel (2026).** Polite but boring? Trade-offs between engagement and psychological reactance to chatbot feedback styles. _CHI 2026_. doi:10.1145/3772318.3790340, https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20683. Read: abstract level only in the check. A behaviour-change feedback experiment, not coaching-specific. Direct feedback gave lower behavioural intentions and higher reactance; politeness gave higher intentions and lower reactance but was unengaging; verbal leakage raised surprise, humour and engagement but also reactance.
6. **Eska, Hensche, Woźniak and Haliburton (2026).** GymBuddy: Embedding motivational theories in conversational AI fitness coaches. _ACM PerDis '26_, 30 Apr 2026. doi:10.1145/3797993.3798020. Read: full text through the ACM page. Four coaching styles from self-determination theory: autonomy-, competence-, relatedness-supportive and controlling. N = 14 (age 21 to 36, 10 female), one lab session of bodyweight squats and sit-ups. The controlling coach was ranked last by 9 of 14 (64.3%); the autonomy- and competence-supportive coaches were never ranked last; the relatedness-supportive coach was never ranked first (too weak, lacking "push"). Some participants said the controlling coach made them try hardest and others felt "restricted"; most wanted friendly plus pushy. The paper included the controlling style because it is still used in sports, not as a negative case, and recommends integrating the supportive styles while balancing controlling behaviours.
7. **Terblanche and Brown (2026).** Rethinking directiveness in AI coaching chatbots. _Frontiers in Psychology_ 17. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1822088. Read: abstract only. Directive vs non-directive generative coaches, N = 158, between-subjects quasi-experiment in workplace coaching (not fitness). The directive coach was rated higher on performance expectancy, working alliance and goal attainment; participants high in extraversion, conscientiousness and openness rated it higher.
8. **Mitchell, Maimone, Cassells, Tobin, Davidson, Smaldone and Mamykina (2021).** Automated vs. human health coaching: Exploring participant and practitioner experiences. _PACM HCI_ 5 (CSCW1). doi:10.1145/3449173, full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9605038/. Read: full text. A scripted coach that always offered a menu of choices was "highly appreciated" by participants, and its tone was seen as supportive and motivational. Human coaches were better at expressing empathy and tailoring advice.

## Synthesis for #80

- Substance first: style helped only when the core advice was sound (1). Those styles also changed the conversational flow, not only the wording, so (1) supports keeping the plan and advice identical across personas but does not test wording-only personas.
- People differ in the style they want (1, 4, 6, 7; one formative participant in 2 asked for a different persona by day), so a user-chosen persona has support; a single fixed house style does not.
- The papers point away from a strict default: facilitative ranked first for most users and above directive in (1), direct feedback raised reactance in (5), and the controlling coach was ranked last by most participants in (6), although some found it pushed them hardest. A directive option has some support (7, in workplace coaching; 1, for users experienced with chatbots, possibly through familiarity), and a question-only style typically lowers engagement (1).
- #80 as written names technical as the default, with encouraging and strict as other styles and strict opt-in only. The papers do not settle the default: supportive styles did well in (1) and (6), and none of the eight tests a calm technical style. Decide the set and the default on #80; candidates are technical, supportive and direct, with strict only as an explicit opt-in.
- Evaluation: compare personas on the same plans with the coach eval (#64), since style effects only show once substance is fixed (1).
